> Here are twelve situations that can kill you, and the one move that actually works in each.

## 1 — Caught in a rip current · 0:25

> You're swimming, and suddenly the beach is getting smaller. That's a rip current — a narrow
> river of water pulling away from shore, and it can move faster than an Olympic swimmer.
> So here's the instinct: swim straight back. And here's what happens — you exhaust yourself in
> about ninety seconds fighting water you can't beat, and exhaustion is what actually drowns people.
> The move: **swim parallel to the beach.** Rips are narrow — often just ten to thirty metres wide.
> Swim sideways, pop out the edge, *then* come in at an angle. Or just float and wave. A rip
> pulls you out. It does not pull you under.

*[VISUALS: top-down animated diagram — red arrow out to sea, green arrow parallel to shore.]*

## 2 — Falling through ice · 1:00

> Cold water doesn't kill you the way you think. Your first problem isn't hypothermia — it's
> the gasp. Hit freezing water and your body involuntarily inhales. Underwater, that's over.
> Remember **one, ten, one.** One minute to get your breathing under control. Ten minutes of
> useful muscle power before your hands stop working. One hour before hypothermia takes you.
> So in that first minute, do nothing but breathe. Then turn back toward the ice you came from —
> that ice held your weight once. Kick your legs up behind you so you're horizontal, pull yourself
> out flat, and **roll away**. Don't stand up. Standing puts all your weight back on one spot.

## 3 — Quicksand · 1:35

> Movies lie about this one. You cannot sink out of sight in quicksand. Your body is less dense
> than the sand-and-water mix — you'll sink to about your waist and stop.
> The danger is being *stuck*: with the tide coming in, in the sun, alone.
> The move: stop struggling — fast movement makes the mix stiffen around you. Lean back, spread
> your weight, and wiggle your legs slowly. That lets water flow back around them and loosens
> the grip. It can take ten minutes. Be boring, and you get out.

## 4 — A bear versus a moose · 2:10

> These need opposite responses, and mixing them up is fatal.
> **Bear: never run.** You cannot outrun it, and running turns you into prey. Stand tall, speak
> in a low voice, back away slowly. If a *black* bear attacks — fight, hit the face and nose.
> If a *brown* bear or grizzly makes contact — play dead, face down, hands behind your neck.
> **Moose: run.** A moose doesn't want to eat you, it wants you gone. It's also a half-tonne
> animal that kills more people per year than bears do. Run — and put a tree between you.
> Moose can't turn quickly around obstacles.

*[VISUALS: side-by-side split screen, red X and green tick over each animal.]*

## 5 — Lightning outdoors · 2:50

> You've seen the advice: crouch down low on the balls of your feet. Weather services **stopped
> recommending that** — it gives you almost no protection.
> The truth is blunt: there is no safe place outside in a thunderstorm. A hard-topped car works
> (the metal shell, not the tyres, routes the current around you). A building works.
> If you're stuck in the open: get off high ground, out of water, away from the single tall tree,
> drop metal poles, and move — keep moving toward real shelter instead of posing in a field.
> And if you can hear thunder, you are already close enough to be hit.

## 6 — Choking, and you're alone · 3:25

> Nobody's coming. You have about a minute of useful consciousness.
> Find a chair, a counter edge, or the back of a sofa. Place it just above your navel, below the
> ribs — then drop your whole body weight onto it, hard and upward. Not a lean. A *slam*.
> Repeat until it clears. It hurts. It works. And before all that: if you can cough at all, cough —
> a cough moves more air than anything you can do with your hands.

## 7 — Your car goes into water · 4:00

> This is the one I promised you. The advice that went viral — *wait for the car to fill up so
> the pressure equalises, then open the door* — is one of the deadliest myths on the internet.
> By the time it equalises you've been underwater for minutes. You don't have minutes.
> You have about sixty seconds while the car floats. Use them in this order: **seatbelt, window, out.**
> Seatbelt off first, before anything. Window down — most power windows work briefly underwater —
> and if it won't go, break the *side* window, not the windshield. Corner of the glass, with a
> headrest prong or a spring-loaded punch.
> Children out first, oldest to youngest, and **don't call anyone**. Not from inside the car.
> Get out, then call.

*[VISUALS: cutaway car animation, on-screen text counter ticking 60 → 0.]*

## 8 — Caught in an avalanche · 4:50

> Snow moving at eighty kilometres an hour sets like concrete the second it stops. So everything
> you do has to happen while it's still moving.
> Fight to stay near the surface — swim, roll, grab anything. Let go of your poles and skis;
> they drag you down. And in the last seconds, before it stops, do the thing that keeps you alive:
> **put both arms in front of your face and push out.** That makes an air pocket, and an air
> pocket is the difference between fifteen minutes and three.
> Buried and disoriented? Spit. Gravity tells you which way is up. Then dig toward the opposite.

## 9 — An aggressive dog · 5:30

> Running triggers the chase. Staring triggers the challenge. Screaming triggers the attack.
> So: stop moving. Turn your body **sideways** — it makes you look smaller and less confrontational.
> Keep the dog in your peripheral vision, never in a direct stare. Arms folded in, hands protected.
> Say nothing, or speak low and flat.
> If it lunges, give it something to bite that isn't you — a bag, a jacket, a shoe. Dogs commit
> to the first thing in their mouth. Then back away while it's busy.

## 10 — Caught in a crowd crush · 6:05

> This one kills more people than almost anything else on this list, and almost nobody is taught it.
> In a dense crowd you don't get trampled — you get compressed. People die standing up, because
> they can't expand their chest to breathe.
> So protect your chest. Bring your arms up in front of it like a boxer, fists near your collarbone,
> elbows out. That reserves a few centimetres of space for your lungs, and those centimetres are your life.
> Stay on your feet. Don't push against the flow — move **diagonally** with it, gaining a little
> ground sideways each surge, toward the edge.
> And feel the ground: if you can't take a full step, the density is already dangerous. Leave early.

*[VISUALS: overhead crowd simulation, one figure highlighted moving diagonally to the edge.]*

## 11 — Lost in the wilderness · 6:50

> The instinct is to walk. Pick a direction, feel productive. And that is exactly how a rescue
> that would have taken four hours turns into four days — because the search area grows with
> every kilometre you cover.
> Use **S.T.O.P.**: Stop. Think. Observe. Plan.
> Then stay put, and make yourself huge. Bright colours spread on the ground. Three of anything —
> three whistle blasts, three fires, three flashes — is the international distress signal.
> Shelter first, then water, then food. You can live three weeks without food. You get about
> three hours in bad weather without shelter.

## 12 — A falling elevator · 7:30

> And the last one, because you've definitely argued about it: should you jump right before it lands?
> No. And here's the honest answer nobody gives you — you cannot jump hard enough to cancel out
> the fall, and you can't time it anyway.
> But you almost certainly don't need to. Modern elevators have multiple cables, magnetic brakes
> that clamp automatically when the cable goes slack, and a buffer at the bottom of the shaft.
> A genuine free-fall is one of the rarest accidents there is.
> If it ever happened: get flat on your back, in the centre of the floor, and cover your head.
> Spreading the force across your whole body beats putting it all through your legs and spine.

## RECAP + CTA — 8:10

> So: swim sideways, not back. Roll off the ice, don't stand. Be slow in quicksand. Run from the
> moose, never from the bear. Seatbelt, window, out. Arms up in the crowd. Stay put when you're lost.
> Almost every one of those is the opposite of instinct — and that's the real lesson here.
> Panic doesn't make you fast. It makes you *predictable*. And in every single one of these
> situations, the predictable move is the wrong one.
>
> Now I want to know: **which one of these did you get wrong?** Be honest — number seven, the car,
> is the one most people have backwards. Tell me in the comments, and if you've ever actually been
> in one of these, I want to read that story.
